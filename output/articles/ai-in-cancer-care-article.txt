HealthX opened this year’s Xeminar series on 9 September with Dr. Zeng Yingchun, Assistant Professor at NUS Nursing and Founder of the FIT Research Team. Her work responds to a pressure point in Singapore’s healthcare system. Cancer diagnoses among people under 40 rose by 34% between 2003–2007 and 2019–2023, even as the population ages, and a growing number of survivors now need support for years after treatment ends. Dr. Zeng’s research asks a sharper question than whether AI can help cancer care. It asks whether AI can help survivors go home, stay independent and return to work, without depending on a workforce the system does not have.

“Start with the clinical problem, let the technology follow.” — Dr. Zeng Yingchun, NUS Nursing

Dr. Zeng illustrated this through the FIT team’s work in virtual reality-based cognitive assessment. Instead of relying solely on conventional pen-and-paper tests, patients navigate familiar everyday tasks in a virtual environment, allowing researchers to capture multiple measures across different cognitive domains. A 2023 study found a correlation above 0.7 with established gold-standard assessments. However, Dr. Zeng emphasised that promising technology should not automatically replace validated tools; VR is being explored as a complement to existing assessments.

The session then turned to three AI tools: Delirium Screening Agent, the LIFE-Review EduAgent, and DyadCare AI, which is designed around the patient and caregiver as a pair. All three are built for palliative and supportive care, where patients need more time than a stretched nursing workforce can give, and all three are meant to hand some of that time back to nurses. None of this work belongs to a single department, and Dr. Zeng was clear that cross-faculty collaboration is what makes it possible.

The session closed with a group task. Teams formed on the spot, each chose one of Singapore’s 7 Asks in cancer care, and had a short window to ideate. What the discussion revealed was a consistent tension. Most of the ideas, from home-based assessment tools to AI-generated rehabilitation plans with a community built around them, ran into the same two questions: cost and trust. Can affordable AI tools exist in the near term? Will patients, already worn down by the number of tests they undergo, accept another? The exercise made the constraints Dr. Zeng works within concrete, and it made her clinical-problem-first principle feel less like a slogan and more like a necessity.

Cancer care extends well beyond treatment itself, into cognition, sleep, delirium, caregiving and the long return to ordinary life. The Xeminar’s central idea was that AI has a place in that longer stretch of care, provided it is built around a real problem, grounded in validated clinical knowledge, and designed to strengthen the work nurses already do. That is a higher bar than most conversations about AI in healthcare set, and it is the reason Dr. Zeng’s work is worth following.
